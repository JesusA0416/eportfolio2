American Institutions (AI) POLS1100

U.S. Govenment & Politics

POLS 1100 – Interest Groups & Media: Politics, but make it fashion. Interest groups are the labels, media is the runway, and policy is the final fit. I learned to check the tag (funding), follow the runway (venue choice), and inspect the stitching (rule text) before I buy the narrative.

Signature Assignment: Interest Group Essay

POLS 1100 Signature Assignment. I analyzed Doctor Patient Unity and learned to ditch hot takes for structure—follow the money, incentives, and rule text. Less scandal, more structure; neutral methods, not neutral vibes.

Reflection

This class was not “the girls are fighting” energy. It was “okay, but how did we get here?” Instead of obsessing over who dragged who this week, we traced the wiring: party coalitions, incentives, money, and the rules that quietly decide who wins. Working on my Doctor Patient Unity essay flipped a switch for me. I came in thinking I was being “neutral,” but neutrality isn’t bland—it’s disciplined. I had to check my tone, rewrite lines that were doing too much, and get comfortable asking boring, powerful questions: Who funds this? What venue are they lobbying? Which clause actually moves the money? Who gets left out when the message says “we’re just protecting patients”?

Process-wise, I got nerdy. I mapped every claim to a source, triangulated across reporting, lobbying disclosures, and policy briefs, and then cut anything I couldn’t back up. I swapped spicy verbs for accurate ones (“argues,” “finds,” “contends”), flagged uncertainty instead of pretending it didn’t exist, and tightened causation so I wasn’t implying more than the evidence could carry. Feedback mattered—I revised to separate what DPU likely influenced from what moved for other reasons. The final draft felt less like a rant and more like… receipts.

The cross-discipline overlap was the fun part. Political science gave me the interest-group playbook (resources, access, venue shopping). Economics explained why surprise billing exists at all—market failure when you can’t shop in an ER—aka a rent-seeking playground. Communication/media showed how a name like “Doctor Patient Unity” launders a corporate campaign into something cuddly. Health policy & ethics forced the equity question: when negotiations stall, who actually pays? And public administration reminded me the fight doesn’t end when a bill passes; it just relocates to agencies and rulemaking.

The bigger mindset shift: I stopped treating politics like gossip and started treating it like infrastructure. People and headlines are loud, but structure—the party incentives, the coalition math, the text of the rule—quietly picks winners and losers. Once you see the plumbing, you can’t unsee it. Now when I hear a claim, I don’t ask “who said it first?” I ask “what system makes this profitable to say?”

Personally, this course made me edit myself in a good way. Even when I wasn’t aiming at anyone, my phrasing carried assumptions. I learned to keep my neutrality method-based: evidence first, language tightened, and my bias on a leash. That travels well outside this class—into health policy, legal analysis, comms, even the way I read news. TL;DR: less scandal, more structure. The drama can trend; the incentives write the ending.
