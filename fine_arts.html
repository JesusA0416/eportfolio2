Fine Arts (FA) – DANC 1010

Dance in Culture

This course explored the power of movement and rhythm as tools of identity, resistance, and storytelling across cultures. We studied dance forms from around the world to understand how physical expression is tied to historical trauma, community celebration, and cultural survival.

Course Description

This course introduces students to dance as a cultural, historical, and artistic form of human expression. Through critical analysis of diverse dance forms, students examine how movement communicates meaning and reflects cultural identity, values, and traditions across the world.

Life History Interview – Katie Cervantes Castillo

When I read Bryan Stevenson’s words about the importance of proximity, I thought of someone who has been close to me for nearly a decade, yet whose experiences differ enough from mine to teach me something new. Katie Cervantes Castillo and I met when we were ten years old, and we have grown together through school, family milestones, and now even working side-by-side in my business. While we share a Mexican-American identity, our upbringings highlight opposite ends of the cultural spectrum. My household is deeply rooted in Catholic traditions, while hers is non-religious. I grew up in a Spanish-speaking home; hers was primarily English-speaking. Despite those differences, she is no less connected to her roots—something that became clearer as we spoke for this interview.

I began by asking Katie how she would describe her culture to someone unfamiliar with it. She paused before answering, “It’s a mix of Mexican traditions and the American lifestyle I grew up in. We’re proud of our heritage, but we don’t live in a super traditional way every day. You’ll see little pieces of Mexico in our lives, but we also fit into mainstream U.S. culture pretty easily.” For her, the connection is strongest through food and family gatherings. Even without the religious structure my family has, her relatives still make time to come together for birthdays, barbecues, or just a casual Sunday afternoon. Speaking Spanish with her grandmother is a ritual that keeps her grounded.

When I asked about the influence of living in the United States on her cultural traditions, Katie explained that adaptation has been constant. “We mix American holidays with Mexican ones,” she told me. “Like, we’ll celebrate the 4th of July with carne asada. And my mom uses more shortcuts in cooking than my grandma ever would.” In her household, “family” extends beyond blood relatives to close family friends, following the Mexican custom of calling trusted adults “tía” or “tío” even without a literal relation. She described her role as the household translator for her grandmother and other relatives—a responsibility that often began in school, where she was expected to speak for her family in English-only environments.

Her description of family roles revealed both familiar and different dynamics from my own. Women typically handle the cooking and planning, but everyone contributes in some way. Support is shown through presence—showing up for celebrations, lending a hand with moves, or simply being there. Discipline comes through lectures and “the look” rather than physical punishment. These details painted a picture of a family that values respect and togetherness, even if their traditions look a little different from mine.

We moved into discussing life milestones. Quinceañeras remain a celebrated marker for young women, along with weddings and landmark birthdays like eighteen or twenty-one. “The quinceañera is probably the most celebrated,” Katie said, “but the early twenties are the most challenging—everyone expects you to have life figured out, and you’re still learning.”

On communication, Katie explained that English is her primary language, but Spanish emerges when speaking to her grandmother or when certain phrases feel more natural in Spanish. She also emphasized the importance of nonverbal communication: greeting elders with a handshake or hug, and making eye contact as a sign of respect. Ignoring a greeting or being too blunt in front of older relatives, she noted, would be considered disrespectful.

>Food came up repeatedly during our conversation. Everyday meals in her home include tacos, enchiladas, and caldo when someone is sick. Tamales, however, carry special weight. “My grandma’s tamales are the most meaningful. She makes them with so much patience, and it’s a family event every Christmas to help her.” This was one of the moments where I recognized a deep overlap in our lives—tamale-making as both cultural preservation and family bonding.

When discussing clothing, Katie described everyday wear as casual—jeans, t-shirts, sneakers—but noted that for special events she might wear dresses, heels, or traditional embroidered blouses. Celebrations such as Christmas, Día de los Muertos, and Mother’s Day stand out in her family. For Día de los Muertos, they build an altar with photos, candles, and marigolds. Music is integral to these moments. Mariachi, banda, cumbia, and norteño fill the air, accompanied by instruments like the guitarrón and accordion.

Katie also shared her perspective on time and gatherings. Social events run on “Mexican time,” with arrivals rarely matching the invitation hour, though punctuality is still important for work and school. Group settings are organized casually, with people naturally gathering around food or music.

Our conversation ended with her experiences in the U.S. “I’ve definitely felt misunderstood or discriminated against,” she admitted. “People sometimes assume I’m undocumented just because I’m Mexican, or they’re surprised I speak perfect English.” In school, she occasionally avoided speaking Spanish to fit in and avoid comments. When I asked what she wished people understood about her experience as a Mexican-American woman, she said, “That we’re not all the same. Every Mexican-American has a different upbringing and connection to the culture, and it’s not always tied to stereotypes like religion, accents, or certain jobs.”

Speaking with Katie reminded me that shared ethnicity does not mean identical experiences. Our stories run parallel in some ways and diverge completely in others, shaped by environment, language, and the subtle pressures of assimilation. Proximity, as Stevenson says, is what allowed me to see those differences clearly and appreciate the resilience behind her cultural duality.

Reflection

What stood out to you about another culture's expierence in living in the United States?

What stood out was how much Katie’s experience as a Mexican-American differed from mine despite our shared heritage. Her non-religious upbringing and primarily English-speaking home shaped her identity in ways I hadn’t experienced, yet she still faced similar societal assumptions about language and immigration status.

What impact did this assignment have on you or your understanding of the world?

It reinforced that cultural identity is complex and layered. Even within the same ethnicity, lived experiences vary widely based on family dynamics, environment, and personal choices. This complexity challenges the idea that one narrative can represent an entire community.

How does this new understanding/information impact your personal sense of cultural competence and global citizenship?

It reminded me to approach conversations without assuming sameness or difference based solely on visible identity. Cultural competence means recognizing both shared roots and individual branches. As a global citizen, this mindset makes me more prepared to engage respectfully with people whose lives overlap with mine in some ways but differ in others.

What similarities did you discover that surprised you?

I was surprised by how much our family food traditions aligned, even though her home life operated under different rules and routines. The shared ritual of making tamales every Christmas showed me that certain traditions can survive generational and linguistic shifts.
