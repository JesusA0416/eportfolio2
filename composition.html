Honors Intermediate Writing (HON 2211)

Project Three

This project represents my work for Honors Intermediate Writing and reflects my engagement with rhetorical awareness, audience, and discourse communities. Through this project, I applied concepts of genre, purpose, and language choice to communicate ideas effectively and thoughtfully.

Disciplinary Languages of Media Regulation: Writing Conventions in the Global Study of Ratings Systems

Introduction

Media rating systems such as the Motion Picture Association of America (MPAA), the Entertainment Software Rating Board (ESRB), and Japan’s Computer Entertainment Rating Organization (CERO) are often discussed in public discourse as consumer guides or regulatory tools. However, within the academic discipline of Media Studies, these systems are analyzed not simply as classifications, but as cultural texts that reflect social values, national identities, and institutional power. This project examines how scholars in Media Studies write about rating systems by analyzing academic texts that focus on media regulation, cultural control, and audience governance. Rather than evaluating the effectiveness of rating systems themselves, this analysis focuses on how knowledge about media regulation is produced and communicated in academic writing. By examining tone, structure, types of evidence, visual practices, and citation conventions across multiple scholarly sources, this essay identifies the key writing conventions that define Media Studies as a discipline. Through this analysis, it becomes clear that Media Studies writing prioritizes critical interpretation, comparative frameworks, and interdisciplinary evidence to contextualize media systems within broader cultural and political structures.

Media Studies as a Field

Media Studies is an interdisciplinary academic field that draws from sociology, cultural studies, communication, law, and political economy. Scholars in this discipline analyze how media texts are produced, regulated, distributed, and consumed, with particular attention to power relations and cultural meaning. Academic writing in Media Studies often treats institutions such as rating boards not as neutral entities, but as ideological systems that shape audience behavior and cultural norms (Johnson, 2015). Unlike technical or policy-oriented disciplines, Media Studies writing is interpretive and argumentative. Authors frequently situate their research within existing theoretical frameworks and use comparative or historical analysis to demonstrate how media practices differ across national and cultural contexts. This disciplinary approach is especially evident in academic discussions of film and video game rating systems.

Tone and Writing Style

Across academic Media Studies texts, the tone is formal, analytical, and critical. Authors rarely adopt persuasive or emotive language; instead, they maintain a scholarly distance while advancing clear interpretive arguments. In From PG-13 to CERO Z: The Transnational Language of Media Regulation, the author uses neutral academic language while critically examining how rating systems encode cultural assumptions about morality, violence, and childhood. Key terms such as “regulatory discourse” and “cultural translation” are defined early, reflecting a discipline that assumes readers may come from multiple academic backgrounds. Similarly, Johnson (2015) employs a measured and objective tone when discussing audience classification, even while challenging the idea that rating systems are culturally neutral. This balance between neutrality and critique is a defining feature of Media Studies writing. Rather than explicitly stating opinions, scholars allow their interpretations to emerge through evidence and theoretical framing.

Structure and Organization

Academic Media Studies articles typically follow a recognizable structure: an introduction that establishes the research question, a literature review or theoretical framework, a main analysis section, and a conclusion that situates the findings within the discipline. However, unlike empirical sciences, Media Studies articles do not always follow rigid methodological sections. Instead, structure is often thematic. In From PG-13 to CERO Z, the article is organized around comparative case studies of rating systems in different countries. Each section examines how cultural values influence classification practices, allowing the reader to see patterns across regions. This comparative structure is common in Media Studies, particularly when analyzing global media systems. Long-form academic essays in this discipline also frequently include subheadings to guide readers through complex arguments. This reflects an awareness that Media Studies audiences may include scholars from law, sociology, or communication studies who benefit from clear organizational cues.

Types of Evidence Used

Media Studies scholarship relies on a diverse range of evidence. Rather than experimental data, authors often use qualitative sources such as policy documents, film examples, historical records, and prior academic research. In discussions of rating systems, scholars frequently analyze official rating guidelines, public controversies, and specific media texts to illustrate how classifications operate in practice. For example, From PG-13 to CERO Z references official MPAA and CERO documentation alongside academic commentary to demonstrate how similar content can be interpreted differently across cultures. This layered use of evidence allows the author to connect institutional language with cultural outcomes. Other academic sources, such as Bryant and Zillmann (2002), provide theoretical context by discussing media effects and audience interpretation. While these texts do not focus exclusively on rating systems, they are used to support broader claims about how audiences interact with regulated media. This reflects a key convention in Media Studies writing: synthesizing theory with applied case studies.

Visualizations and Multimodal Elements

Visual elements in Media Studies academic writing are used sparingly but strategically. Charts, tables, or screenshots may be included to illustrate rating categories, comparative systems, or historical timelines. However, visualizations are typically supplementary rather than central to the argument. In many Media Studies articles, visuals serve as reference points rather than data presentations. This contrasts with disciplines such as geography or psychology, where graphs and quantitative figures are essential. The limited use of visuals reflects the discipline’s emphasis on textual and interpretive analysis.

Citation Practices and Research Scope

Media Studies scholarship commonly uses APA or MLA citation styles, depending on whether the article leans toward social science or humanities traditions. Citations are frequent and integrated directly into the text, reinforcing the discipline’s emphasis on engaging with existing scholarship. One of the key expectations in Media Studies writing is extensive engagement with prior research. Scholarly articles typically reference multiple academic sources to situate arguments within ongoing conversations. In the revised version of this project, academic sources are used not only to provide information about rating systems, but also to demonstrate how Media Studies scholars frame and analyze those systems.

Conclusion

Conclusion

Through the analysis of academic texts on media rating systems, this project demonstrates that Media Studies writing is characterized by formal tone, thematic structure, qualitative evidence, restrained use of visuals, and rigorous citation practices. Scholars in this discipline approach rating systems as cultural and ideological constructs rather than neutral tools, using comparative and theoretical analysis to reveal how media regulation reflects broader social values. Understanding these disciplinary conventions has clarified how Media Studies scholars communicate knowledge and engage with complex media systems. This awareness also informs my own academic writing, particularly my ability to frame arguments, integrate sources, and adapt tone for a scholarly audience. By learning to write within these conventions, I am better prepared to participate in academic conversations within Media Studies and related fields.

Course Reflection

This course reshaped how I think about writing, not as something that simply demonstrates knowledge, but as a form of action shaped by audience, context, and power. Before this semester, I viewed writing primarily as a product: an essay, an argument, or an assignment to be graded. Through Honors Intermediate Writing, I began to see writing as a process that reflects discourse communities, values, and expectations, and as a skill that adapts depending on who is reading and why the writing exists in the first place.

One of the most important things I learned about writing as a discipline is that there is no single correct way to write well. Instead, effective writing depends on understanding rhetorical situation, including audience, purpose, genre, and context. Analyzing discourse communities helped me recognize how language choices signal belonging, credibility, and authority. This was especially meaningful for me because my academic and personal experiences often require moving between different spaces such as academic, professional, and personal environments, each with its own expectations for tone, structure, and voice.

The most meaningful concept we explored this semester was the idea that language is not neutral. Word choice, structure, and even silence communicate values. This became especially clear when we examined how writing can reinforce or challenge systems of power. Writing stopped feeling like a purely academic exercise and instead became a way to engage responsibly with the world.

The most challenging part of this course was revising my own work. Revision required me to slow down and critically examine my writing beyond surface- level edits. I learned to use feedback more intentionally and to reconsider structure, clarity, and purpose. This process helped me understand that revision is a core component of effective writing, not a weakness.

The skills I developed in this class will transfer to other courses and to my life beyond college. Understanding audience expectations, adapting tone, and reflecting on my own communication choices are skills I will continue to use in academic, professional, and personal settings.

This semester required balancing coursework with responsibilities outside the classroom, but this course provided a space where my experiences as a learner mattered. Honors Intermediate Writing reinforced that writing is not just about meeting requirements, but about developing a voice that can grow and adapt.
