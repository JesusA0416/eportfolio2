World Regional Geography

This section contains coursework and spatial analysis completed for World Regional Geography. The focus of this portfolio is regional patterns, global processes, and geographic interpretation using maps and data visualization tools.

History of Pandemics

Scientists have identified traces of ancient viruses preserved within the DNA of hosts, often described as molecular fossils. These remnants allow researchers to track how viruses evolved over time, how they adapted to new hosts, and how viral transmission changed alongside human migration and population growth.

Throughout history, several pandemics have permanently altered global society, including the Black Death, smallpox, the 1918 influenza pandemic, HIV/AIDS, cholera, and COVID-19. These diseases reshaped population distribution, economic systems, and public health responses.

Pandemics spread through person-to-person transmission and global connectivity, including trade routes, air travel, and urban density. The 1918 influenza pandemic was considered the “mother of all pandemics” because of its rapid global spread and extraordinarily high death toll, exacerbated by misinformation and lack of transparency.

Modern vaccination strategies rely on training the immune system to recognize pathogens through exposure to weakened or inactive viral components. This approach has helped control diseases such as smallpox and polio and plays a critical role in managing pandemics like COVID-19.

Epidemics vs Pandemics

An epidemic refers to a disease outbreak that affects a specific region or population, while a pandemic involves widespread global transmission across multiple continents. On March 11, 2020, the World Health Organization officially declared COVID-19 a pandemic due to its rapid global spread.

COVID-19 spread through asymptomatic transmission and community spread, making containment more difficult. Social distancing was implemented to reduce transmission by limiting close contact and slowing the rate of infection.

The epidemiological strategy known as “flattening the curve” aimed to reduce the peak number of infections at any given time, preventing healthcare systems from becoming overwhelmed. Geographic differences in policy responses led to varied outcomes across regions.

Origins of COVID-19

COVID-19 is believed to have originated in Wuhan, China, potentially linked to wildlife-human interaction at wet markets. Wet markets in China differ from those in other regions due to the presence of live wildlife species sold for consumption.

China’s Wildlife Protection Law allowed wildlife farming, which increased human exposure to zoonotic diseases. Changes to this industry following COVID-19 highlighted the risks associated with wildlife trade.

The World Health Organization emphasized the importance of naming diseases without geographic or cultural references to avoid stigmatization. COVID-19 was named based on the year it was discovered rather than its location of origin to reduce racist or xenophobic associations.

Spatial and Historical Distribution

Interactive GIS maps from Johns Hopkins University and The New York Times reveal the uneven spatial distribution of COVID-19 across the globe. Regions with high population density and global connectivity, such as the United States, Europe, and parts of Asia, experienced the highest case counts.

Within the United States, early outbreaks were concentrated in urban centers before spreading nationwide. Data trends show that while cases in mainland China stabilized after strict containment measures, cases in other regions continued to rise due to delayed responses.

The spatial analysis highlights how geography, policy decisions, and population movement influenced the trajectory of the pandemic.

Racism, Misinformation, and Public Health

The COVID-19 pandemic was accompanied by increased racist rhetoric, particularly toward Asian communities. Misinformation and xenophobia spread alongside the virus, undermining public health efforts.

Common misinformation included false claims about transmission methods, prevention strategies, and vaccine safety. Distinguishing scientific guidance from fear-driven misinformation became critical.

Simple public health measures such as handwashing are effective because soap disrupts the lipid membrane of the virus, rendering it inactive. Individuals can help protect society by following public health guidelines, sharing accurate information, and supporting community health efforts.
